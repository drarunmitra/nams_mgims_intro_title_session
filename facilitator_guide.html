1. What people should leave with

  • An Introduction is a funnel: known, unknown (the gap), why it matters, objective.
  • The last sentence is a specific objective that matches Methods and Results.
  • No history lesson, no literature dump, no methods, no results.
  • One claim, one verified citation. A real reference can still be the wrong reference.
  • AI can draft text, but you must check every sentence and every reference.
  • A title names population, setting and design, and has no hype.

The running example is synthetic. A cross-sectional study of 312 urinary E. coli isolates (first isolate per patient), tertiary hospital, Telangana. ESBL 158/312 (50.6%). It is the same example used in Session 4 (Results) on Day 1. Say "illustrative" out loud at least once.

4. Activity 1: Spot the Problem (12–22)

Set-up. Pairs read the flawed Introduction (slide 11, also on the handout) and mark every problem. Tell them there are seven. Do not show the list until the 4 minutes are up.

Answer key: the seven planted problems

#ProblemWhere in the textWhy it is a problem
1Cliché opener"among the most common infections known to mankind"Says nothing specific; no source.
2History lessonFleming and penicillin, 1928Textbook history. The reader knows it.
3Uncited statistic"About half of all women will have a UTI in their lifetime"A number with no source. (Foxman 2002 supports it, but it is US data from the 1990s.)
4Literature dumpSharma, Reddy, Singh, Kumar, Patel "et al."A list, no synthesis, no point. Names are placeholders.
5No explicit gapNothing says what is missingThe objective then feels random.
6Vague objective"to study the pattern of antimicrobial resistance"No population, outcome or design. "Study to study" is also clumsy.
7Result revealed"we found that 50.6% … were ESBL producers"A result belongs in Results.

Bonus catches (praise them, do not require them): "Antibiotic resistance is a big problem in India" is vague and uncited; ESBL is never defined; "Hence" claims a logic that is not there; the text has no paragraph breaks.

The Rewrite tab

Four short paragraphs: known, ESBL, gap and why it matters, then one bold objective that mirrors Session 4 (ESBL proportion, six antibiotics, associated factors). The four citations are real and were opened (Foxman 2002; Flores-Mireles 2015; Pitout & Laupland 2008; ICMR AMRSN Annual Report 2024). The gap sentence ("we found no recent first-isolate data from our region") is illustrative. In a real paper the author must search the literature first.

Do not say that the rewrite is "the" correct Introduction. It is one good version. Ask the room what they would change.

5. The AI demo: what to say and show (26–33)

This is a real test, not staged. The same prompt was run nine times on 23 November 2026 with no tools and no web: 6 on a small Claude model, 3 on a larger one. Raw outputs are in ai_demo/.

What it found

  • First run (slides 18–19): all five references are real. Three do not support the sentence they sit under: Murray 2022 (overstated), Bush 2010 (weak fit), Gupta 2011 (a US/EU cystitis guideline cited for an India claim). Judged from titles and abstracts only.
  • Nine runs (slide 20): 45 references. 43 of 43 journal references exist in PubMed (13 different papers). Two Opus runs cite an ICMR 2022 report that was not opened. No fabricated journal paper was found.
  • Three of the nine runs say Indian ESBL prevalence "exceeds half". None cited an Indian urinary ESBL prevalence study for it (general reviews or policy papers, judged from titles). Six of nine end with a blank "[setting]".

Do not tell the room that AI always invents references. It did not here. Say it can (published rates: 55% for GPT-3.5 and 18% for GPT-4, Walters & Wilder 2023; 47% for GPT-3.5, Bhattacharyya 2023; abstracts only, older models), and that a real reference can still be the wrong reference. That is the lesson.

The planted fake (slide 21)

The sixth reference (Narayanan & Deshpande, J Glob Antimicrob Resist 2023;33:211-9) is invented by us, not by the AI. It is labelled in red. Type its title into PubMed live and show the empty result. Say clearly: "We made this one up so you can practise catching it."

Other flaws to point out in the AI text

  • The gap is generic: "data … remain fragmented".
  • The main India claim has no citation.
  • Citation numbers are out of order ([1], [3,4], [3], [2], [5]).
  • The objective has a blank "[setting]".

Caveats to say out loud

  • Each call was told to answer from memory, so the later runs end with "verify these" notes. A chatbot used the usual way may not add them.
  • Other tools and other days will give other results.
  • Existence was checked by script (ai_demo/check_refs.py). "Supports the claim" was judged by hand for the first run and, by title, for the "exceeds half" sentence. Full texts were not read.

Slide 22: using AI safely

One minute only. Give the AI your verified sources; ask for structure, language and gap-check questions; check every sentence; disclose as the journal asks. Ethics detail belongs to Session 6 (Day 2, 09:30).

6. Activity 2: Title Makeover (33–42)

Set-up. The handout has the five weak titles and blank space. Show them one at a time. Give 30 seconds, take two or three answers, then reveal the fix. Fixes are examples, not the only answer.

#Weak titleWhat is missingFixed version (on the slide)

  • Titles 1–5 are teaching examples written for this deck, not real papers. The dressing trial is entirely invented.
  • Title 2: the fix assumes a diagnostic accuracy design (STARD). The real fix depends on the real design.
  • Title 3: Wardha is chosen because the workshop is at MGIMS Sevagram. It is not a real study.
  • Title 5: the fix keeps "randomised" (CONSORT 2025 item 1a).

Makeover of the running example (slide 31)

Start from "Antibiotic resistance pattern of E. coli". Ask for each part: organism, specimen, setting, outcomes, design. Land on: ESBL production and antibiotic resistance in urinary Escherichia coli isolates at a tertiary hospital in Telangana: a cross-sectional study. Challenge them to cut five words without losing the design (for example drop "isolates").

Guideline wording (checked). STROBE 1a: "Indicate the study's design with a commonly used term in the title or the abstract". CONSORT 2025 1a: "Identification as a randomised trial". ICMJE: "The title provides a distilled description of the complete article." ICMJE does not say "concise".

Evidence on titles is mixed and observational. Shorter titles and results-stating titles were cited more (Letchford 2015; Paiva 2012); question titles were downloaded more but cited less (Jamali 2011). Say "associated with", never "causes". Do not let this override the reporting guidelines.

7. Running late or early

If you are behind (cut in this order)

  1. Slide 25 (three title styles): say it in one sentence and skip the evidence aside. Saves 1 minute.
  2. Slide 15 (verify, then manage): read the four steps aloud. Saves 1 minute.
  3. Title 4 of 5 (slide 29): skip it. Saves 1 minute.
  4. Slide 20 (nine runs): give the three headline numbers only. Saves 1 minute.

Never cut Activity 1, the planted-fake check, or the final checklist.

If you are ahead

  • Ask pairs to write an objective sentence for their own study and swap.
  • Ask the room to rewrite the AI text's last paragraph with a specific gap.
  • Take more questions on citing and AI use.

8. Questions you may get

Can I use AI to write my Introduction?

It can draft or tighten text you give it. You find and read the sources, check each sentence against a page you hold, and disclose as the journal asks. Never ask it for references and paste them in. Policy detail is Session 6.

How many references should an Introduction have?

No fixed number. ICMJE says to cite only directly pertinent references. One claim, one citation. Check the journal's limit.

How long should it be?

Guides disagree: Bahadoran (2018) gives 2–3 paragraphs and about 250–300 words; Cals & Kotz (2013) say "four to five paragraphs" and under 10–15% of the paper. The deck's 300–500 words is a teaching range. The journal's limit wins.

Can the Introduction mention my results?

No. ICMJE: "do not include data or conclusions from the work being reported." The result goes in Results.

Do I need a gap statement if the topic is routine surveillance?

Yes, but it can be modest: no recent first-isolate data from your region, or none from your setting. Search the literature before you write it. Say what the data will change.

Is the CARS model required?

No. It is a model of how introductions are built, with three moves: establish a territory, a niche, then occupy the niche. It is the same funnel with names. The move names come from secondary sources; Swales' books were not opened.

Are question titles allowed?

Some journals discourage them. In the one study checked (Jamali 2011, abstract only) they were downloaded more but cited less. Check the journal.

Which ICMR report should I cite?

The exact edition you opened. The Annual Report 2024 (8th edition) was opened. Nothing newer was found, but the whole ICMR index was not browsed.
