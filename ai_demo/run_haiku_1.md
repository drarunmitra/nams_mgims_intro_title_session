**Introduction**

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the most common bacterial infections worldwide, and *Escherichia coli* causes most community-acquired and hospital-acquired cases. Over the past two decades, extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL)-producing *E. coli* has become a major threat to empirical therapy. These enzymes, most often of the CTX-M type, hydrolyse third-generation cephalosporins and aztreonam (1). The genes are frequently carried on plasmids that also encode resistance to fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole, which leaves few oral options (3).

Globally, the proportion of *E. coli* urinary isolates producing ESBLs has risen steadily, and community carriage has spread alongside hospital-associated strains (2). Infections caused by these organisms are linked to higher rates of treatment failure, longer hospital stays and greater mortality than infections caused by susceptible strains (4).

India carries a heavy burden. High rates of antibiotic use, both prescribed and over the counter, uneven infection prevention in many facilities, and a large informal drug market are widely thought to drive resistance. Reported ESBL prevalence among urinary *E. coli* isolates from Indian tertiary centres often exceeds half of the isolates tested, although estimates vary across regions, settings and laboratory methods (5). That variation makes local surveillance data essential for guiding empirical treatment.

Data from community and peri-urban populations, and from routine diagnostic laboratories outside large metropolitan hospitals, remain limited. Local estimates of prevalence, dominant ESBL genotypes and co-resistance patterns are needed to inform treatment guidelines and stewardship programmes.

We therefore aimed to estimate the prevalence of ESBL production among *E. coli* isolated from urine samples in [study setting], India, to describe their antimicrobial susceptibility profiles, and to identify factors associated with ESBL carriage.
