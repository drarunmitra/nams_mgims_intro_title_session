**Introduction (about 300 words)**

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the most common bacterial infections worldwide and a leading reason for antibiotic prescribing in India. Escherichia coli is the dominant uropathogen, causing most community-acquired and hospital-acquired UTIs. Over the past two decades, extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) production has become one of the most important resistance mechanisms in Enterobacterales. ESBLs confer resistance to third-generation cephalosporins and aztreonam, and they often occur alongside resistance to fluoroquinolones and aminoglycosides.[1,2]

Antimicrobial resistance is a major global health threat. Bacterial AMR was directly responsible for an estimated 1.27 million deaths in 2019.[3] The burden is especially concerning in South Asia, where high antibiotic consumption, gaps in infection prevention, and dense healthcare and community transmission networks favour the spread of resistant clones.[4] Kumarasamy and colleagues showed how rapidly resistance determinants can move across borders in the region.[5]

Empirical treatment of UTIs in India often proceeds without current local susceptibility data. Inappropriate empirical therapy can lead to treatment failure, prolonged illness, and further selection pressure for resistance. ESBL prevalence in urinary E. coli varies widely across regions, between community and hospital settings, and by level of care. Recent data from community-based samples remain limited. Local prevalence, risk factors and susceptibility profiles are therefore needed to guide empirical therapy and antimicrobial stewardship.

This study aims to (i) determine the prevalence of ESBL production among E. coli urinary isolates in [study setting], (ii) identify associated patient and clinical risk factors, and (iii) describe antimicrobial susceptibility patterns, including carbapenem and non-β-lactam agents. The findings will inform local empirical treatment guidelines and stewardship interventions.
