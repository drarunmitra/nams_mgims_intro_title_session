**Introduction**

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the most common bacterial infections in both community and hospital settings. They cause a large share of outpatient visits and empirical antibiotic prescriptions (1). *Escherichia coli* is the leading uropathogen and causes most uncomplicated and many complicated UTIs (1). Treatment is becoming harder because more *E. coli* isolates produce extended-spectrum beta-lactamases (ESBLs). These enzymes hydrolyse penicillins, third-generation cephalosporins and aztreonam, and they are often carried on plasmids together with genes for resistance to fluoroquinolones, aminoglycosides and co-trimoxazole (2). The CTX-M family, especially CTX-M-15, now dominates worldwide and spreads in the community as well as in hospitals (2).

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) caused an estimated 1.27 million deaths directly in 2019, and *E. coli* was the pathogen responsible for the largest number of resistance-associated deaths (3). South Asia carries a disproportionate share of this burden. In India, several factors drive resistance: high antibiotic use, over-the-counter access to antibiotics, self-medication, crowded health facilities, and poor sanitation that allows resistant enteric organisms to move between people and the environment (4). Surveillance through the Indian Council of Medical Research's Antimicrobial Resistance Surveillance and Research Network has recorded high third-generation cephalosporin resistance among Enterobacterales in Indian tertiary care centres (5).

Even so, local data on ESBL-producing *E. coli* in urinary isolates are still uneven. Most reports come from single tertiary hospitals, use different phenotypic confirmation methods, and seldom separate community-onset from hospital-onset infection. Clinicians who choose empirical therapy, and committees that write institutional antibiograms, need current susceptibility patterns from their own region. Without these data, prescribers tend to default to carbapenems, which speeds up the selection of carbapenem resistance.

This study therefore estimated the prevalence of ESBL production among *E. coli* urinary isolates at [setting], described their susceptibility to commonly used oral and parenteral agents, and examined patient factors associated with ESBL carriage. The aim was to inform empirical UTI treatment and antimicrobial stewardship.
