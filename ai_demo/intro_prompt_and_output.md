Introduction

Urinary tract infection (UTI) is among the most common bacterial infections worldwide, and Escherichia coli is its predominant cause, accounting for most community-acquired and a substantial share of hospital-acquired cases [1]. Uncomplicated cystitis and pyelonephritis drive a large volume of outpatient antibiotic prescribing, which makes UTI management a central driver of antimicrobial use.

Extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) production allows E. coli to hydrolyse third-generation cephalosporins and aztreonam, leaving clinicians fewer oral options and often pushing them toward carbapenems or other reserve agents [3,4]. ESBL genes, most commonly CTX-M types, are typically carried on plasmids that transfer readily between strains, enabling dissemination across community and healthcare settings. Pitout and Laupland described ESBL-producing Enterobacteriaceae as an emerging public-health concern [3]. Global burden estimates place antimicrobial resistance among the leading contributors to infection-related deaths, with the greatest pressure where antibiotic access is weakly regulated [2].

India reports high proportions of ESBL-producing E. coli among urinary isolates, but prevalence estimates vary widely across institutions and regions. This variation reflects differences in patient populations, prior antibiotic exposure, catheterisation and laboratory methods, and it limits the usefulness of single-centre estimates for guiding treatment. Empirical choices, including fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins and nitrofurantoin, are often made without local susceptibility data [5]. Co-resistance to fluoroquinolones and aminoglycosides is common among ESBL producers, further narrowing the remaining options.

Data on ESBL E. coli from Indian urinary isolates remain fragmented, and few studies link resistance phenotypes and genotypes to clinical outcomes or to community versus hospital origin. A clear description of prevalence, susceptibility and co-resistance patterns is needed to inform empirical prescribing and infection-control policy. We therefore conducted a study to estimate the prevalence of ESBL production among E. coli urinary isolates, characterise their antimicrobial susceptibility and co-resistance profiles, and identify associated risk factors in [setting].
