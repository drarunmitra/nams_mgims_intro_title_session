**Introduction**

Urinary tract infection (UTI) is one of the most common bacterial infections worldwide and a leading reason for outpatient antibiotic prescribing. *Escherichia coli* causes most community-acquired and a substantial share of hospital-acquired UTIs. Its growing resistance to third-generation cephalosporins, mainly through extended-spectrum beta-lactamase (ESBL) production, has changed empirical treatment and narrowed options for patients with serious infection.

ESBLs are plasmid-encoded enzymes that hydrolyse penicillins, oxyimino-cephalosporins and aztreonam, and are inhibited by clavulanate. Since their first description, they have spread from hospitals into community reservoirs, and ESBL-producing *E. coli* is now a major concern for public health (1,2). Global burden estimates attribute a large number of deaths each year to bacterial antimicrobial resistance, with Gram-negative pathogens contributing a disproportionate share (3). Resistance in urinary isolates matters particularly because UTI is so frequent and because oral options such as nitrofurantoin, fosfomycin and pivmecillinam are limited to lower urinary tract infection.

India has a high burden of infectious disease, widespread over-the-counter antibiotic use, and large variation in diagnostic capacity between public and private facilities. These conditions favour the selection and transmission of resistant organisms. Surveillance reports and hospital studies from India have described high rates of ESBL production among urinary *E. coli* isolates, often exceeding half of tested isolates in tertiary centres, but data from community settings, and from different regions, remain fragmented (4,5). Such variation limits the ability to set empirical guidelines.

Several gaps persist. Many studies are single-centre, use inconsistent phenotypic confirmation methods, and do not link resistance to prior antibiotic exposure or to community versus hospital origin. Few describe the co-resistance patterns that determine oral treatment options.

This study addresses these gaps by estimating the prevalence of ESBL production among *E. coli* isolated from urine samples in [setting], describing its association with patient and healthcare characteristics, and characterising co-resistance to commonly used oral agents. The findings are intended to inform empirical prescribing and infection-control policy.
